Pioneer MKII: Underwater Vehicle Redesign

Redesigning Pioneer for improved sensing, serviceability, hydrodynamics and field handling. Within ITU ROV Team I worked as mechanical and product designer; the inner and outer design of the nose module were mine.

RoleMechanical & Product Designer
TeamITU ROV Team · 2022–2023

01, Problem

Two conflicting needs, one module

Pioneer MKII is a blind-dive exploration vehicle that runs sonar- and camera-based underwater imaging and mapping tasks, in both remote-controlled and autonomous mode. Its nose module had to meet two needs in tension at the same time:

  • Sonar, camera and sensor components staying in a fixed, accurate position under underwater vibration and pressure
  • The hull moving in a low-drag form, while the same sensors can still sense the outside environment optically and acoustically

These two requirements often push form in opposite directions: structural rigidity wants a closed, thick section; sensor visibility wants an open, thin one.

02, Design Brief

From a working platform to a more capable system

The redesign focused on increasing sensing capability while improving the vehicle architecture for maintenance, flow and handling. My contribution covered five areas:

  1. Sensor packaging: a redesigned internal sensor holder for the head compartment, integrating sonar and cameras, including an additional front sonar.
  2. Head compartment: outer shell redesign with optimized sensor openings and a hydrodynamic form.
  3. Structural design: component layout, structural integration and packaging.
  4. Hydrodynamics: refined outer geometry for improved flow and reduced drag.
  5. Serviceability: modular design for easier maintenance, part replacement and transport.

04, Design Development

Exploration sketches

Early studies explored component packaging, shell geometry, sensor integration and service access.

Blunt and conical nose forms were evaluated. The conical/torpedo form proved better suited both for drag coefficient and for the sensors' unobstructed forward field of view. The fins were placed symmetrically at the rear of the hull to support the vehicle's directional stability underwater.

05, Sensor Architecture

Redesigning the head compartment for expanded sensing

New sensing requirements drove a complete redesign of the internal sensor architecture, integrating two cameras and additional acoustic sensing within the available package. The carrier went from the previous sensor mount with limited packaging space, through a redesigned compact structural carrier, to an integrated package with a front camera, a bottom camera and three sonar sensors.

The internal bracket holding the sonar, camera and sensor components was designed to meet two needs at once: fastening the components securely and being removable for maintenance. The bracket was built as a modular sub-structure that seats into the hull's inner cylinder.

On the outer shell side, the hull was shaped with open regions left clear so as not to cut the sensors' line of sight; section thickness was tuned locally between structural rigidity and the need for openness.

06, Internal Architecture

Making the system legible

Transparent studies were used to evaluate packaging relationships between sensing, propulsion and the pressure housing: the head compartment for sensor and camera packaging, the central housing as the protected system volume, the thruster assembly for maneuvering and the internalized structural path.

The outer shell and inner bracket were developed in parallel as two mutually constraining parts, with every change to the internal layout the outer form was reshaped too. The process was supported by exploded-view modeling to validate assembly logic before production.

07, Structure + Flow

Cleaning the external envelope

The middle-compartment relationship was reconsidered around rigidity, assembly access and a cleaner flow path. The M8 tie rods moved inside the body envelope: they keep the structural load path while reducing exposed hardware and creating a cleaner profile.

08, Serviceability

Designing for disassembly

The architecture was developed to reduce friction during access, replacement and reassembly of modular sections: modular access, component replacement, simpler reassembly.

For assembly, the bracket was kept modular so it could be removed and re-installed by hand (DFA); sensor replacement or maintenance doesn't require opening the whole hull.

09, Stability & Materials

Lightweight stabilizing surfaces

The tail surfaces were redesigned around stability, stiffness and lower mass. Carbon fiber tails: composite fins provide a lightweight and rigid solution for the vehicle's stabilizing surfaces.

10, Final Exterior

A technical object with a cleaner identity

The final enclosure resolves the internal architecture into a compact, readable external form. Structural elements and internal components are contained within the enclosure, leaving only the thrusters and stabilizers exposed outside the main body.

11, DFM / DFA

A manufacturing constraint became a design decision

The orange color was a deliberate design decision for visibility and safety underwater. But during production, under pressure to make the TEKNOFEST deadline, we had to continue with the FDM filament on hand, the final physical prototype was produced in a different color.

12, Prototyping

Physical validation with FDM 3D printing

The design was manufactured by FDM 3D printing and assembled with the real electronic and sensor components.

13, Testing

Pool test

The vehicle was tested under real pool conditions, both its motion stability and the sensor package's physical integrity were confirmed.

14, Iteration

A limited but real feedback loop

The color decision (described in the DFM section) is this project's most concrete iteration example. Other geometric revisions were made during the CAD stage, before moving to physical printing, so this wasn't a series of physical prototype iterations but a single, validated production run.

15, Engineering Overview

Solid outside. Structured inside.

A compact summary of the redesign decisions that define Pioneer MKII: sensor integration, structural packaging, serviceability, hydrodynamic refinement, composite stabilizers, handling and transport.

16, ITU ROV Team · 2022–2023

One year of designing, building and competing

A year of hands-on development with the ITU ROV Team, from design and engineering decisions to assembly, testing and competition.

Results: Pioneer MKII received the TEKNOFEST Design & Presentation Award; the team's Explorer vehicle placed 3rd at the TAC Challenge in Norway.

17, Also on the team

Explorer: underwater vehicle project

I contributed to Explorer with design proposals focused on a lightweight, robust and highly maneuverable vehicle architecture, with particular attention to easy assembly and disassembly. I also took an active role in design reviews and team discussions.

18, Outcome

TEKNOFEST, Design and Presentation Award

In one stage of the competition, a blind dive was attempted with sonar sensors whose testing was not finished, in a push for extra points. It did not deliver the expected score, so the vehicle placed outside the overall ranking. Even so, Pioneer MKII was recognized in TEKNOFEST's Design and Presentation category.
