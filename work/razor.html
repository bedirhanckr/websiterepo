Philips MG7720: A Redesign for Repairability

When the battery dies, a fifty to hundred dollar shaver gets thrown away, even though the part that actually needs replacing is a few dollars' worth of battery. This project goes to the root of the problem: lack of access, tools and trust.

RoleIndividual product redesign
ProductPhilips MG7720 cordless shaver
ResultFoam prototype, final design with two color variants
MethodTeardown, foam prototyping, CAD
ToolsRhino
Problem Research Criteria Concept Sketch CAD Engineering DFM/DFA Prototype Iteration Final
01, Problem

It's not the battery that gets thrown away, it's the whole device

When a cordless shaver's battery capacity drops or dies completely, most users buy a new machine outright instead of attempting to open it up. Yet the failed part is a small component of the device: the internal battery.

Students were defined as the target user group: limited budget, no specialized disassembly tools on hand, no experience opening electronic devices, but not unfamiliar with technology either. For this group, throwing away a fifty to hundred dollar device over a few dollars' worth of battery makes no sense economically or environmentally.

02, Research

Starting by disassembling the real device

To make the problem concrete, a real Philips MG7720 and its charging adapter were disassembled by hand. Where the battery sits inside the body, what screw type secures it (Torx), and where the cover clips in were examined one by one. This inspection answered the question "can the user safely open this device without a tool": no.

03, Design Criteria

How we decided what was right

CriterionRequirement
AccessibilityBattery compartment must open without a special tool
SafetyUser must not accidentally contact electronic components
Water resistanceSealing must hold up under bathroom use conditions
Charging experienceCharge state and dock alignment must be visually clear
ManufacturabilityMust stay compatible with existing injection molding methods
EncouragementThe user should be encouraged that they can change the battery themselves; the battery should be positioned to feel more accessible
04, Concept Development

Focusing on the battery and charging experience

The concept focused on three axes: repairability, up-cycling and a new storage-and-charging proposal. The back of the body was redefined as a separate, hand-openable cover, so the battery can be swapped easily and the device lasts longer. To stop devices from scattering around while charging, a safe and tidy storage-and-charging station (a dock) was designed; the dock also shows visually that the device is correctly aligned.

Two color variants (black and blue) were developed in parallel. This wasn't a functional difference, it was a deliberate exercise to compare material and surface decisions.

05, Sketch

First ideas straight out of the teardown

The first cover and screw-placement ideas were developed right after the teardown analysis, through the cross-section sketches included in the working board in the Problem section. A separate, standalone sketch set for this project wasn't archived in a way that could be added to this case study.

06, CAD Process

Modeling two color variants in parallel

The body and the removable battery cover were modeled in CAD. The black and blue variants were produced from the same geometry, with only material and surface settings changed. This approach set out to show how a single mold design can be adapted to different market segments.

07, Engineering Decisions

A removable cover, with real dimensions

The battery cover's wall thickness, clip tolerance and gasket seating surface were each defined in the technical drawing. The goal was a cover that could be opened safely by hand and still close without compromising water resistance.

08, DFM / DFA

Repairability became an assembly decision

In the original device, reaching the battery required opening the entire body. In the redesign, the battery cover was separated into an independent sub-assembly that could be removed and reinstalled on its own. This decision improved both independent testability during production and user-side maintainability.

09, Prototyping

Validating form through foam

Form decisions were validated first with a rough volume study, then with a dimensioned, detailed foam model. The foam model here isn't a functional prototype, it's a form study used to test grip and proportions.

10, Iteration

From rough volume to final proportion

The first foam study was deliberately left rough to test shave depth and grip angle. The second foam model was brought closer to the final proportion with dimensional corrections that came out of the first study. The color variant decision (black / blue) was handled during the CAD stage, as a material and surface comparison.
